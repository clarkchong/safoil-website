In May 2025, Germany's biofuels regulator, the BLE, [linked two companies to its latest biodiesel fraud investigation](https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/crude-oil/050725-german-biofuels-regulator-links-two-companies-to-certification-fraud). One detail caught the attention of the whole trade. A Dutch supplier connected to the case still held a valid ISCC certificate, yet the regulator had "considerable doubts" that it existed at all. The certificate was real. The paperwork looked in order. The company behind it, apparently, was not.

That is the uncomfortable lesson of the past two years. In a market where waste-based fuels earn a premium, a certificate on file is where due diligence starts, not where it ends. Here are seven questions that go further.

## Why the stakes have risen

The incentive is simple. Under European rules, fuel made from waste and residues counts for more than fuel made from crops, so a cargo labelled as used cooking oil or POME is worth more than the same oil labelled as virgin.

In December 2024, Transport & Environment, a Brussels campaign group, [published an analysis](https://www.transportenvironment.org/articles/uco) of ISCC-certified UCO collectors in China, Malaysia and Indonesia, the three biggest exporters of UCO to Europe. It found that only 9 percent of those collecting points had a sample of their points of origin verified by an audit. Whether or not you agree with its conclusions, the figure explains why regulators are paying attention. As a Malaysian supplier ourselves, we take it as a reason to make our own paperwork easy to check.

The certifiers are responding. ISCC is by far the largest scheme in this market: it [certified 94 percent of all UK renewable fuel in 2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/renewable-transport-fuel-obligation-rtfo-statistics-2024-final-report/renewable-transport-fuel-obligation-rtfo-statistics-2024-final-report), and held more than 7,500 valid certificates by early 2025. In August 2025 it [launched a working group on the chemical analysis of feedstocks](https://shipandbunker.com/news/world/957493-iscc-launches-new-working-group-to-boost-feedstock-verification), starting with used cooking oil. It has also [said it will withdraw certificates](https://www.argusmedia.com/pt/news-and-insights/latest-market-news/2441623-iscc-responds-to-suspected-biodiesel-mislabelling) from companies supplying volumes of advanced feedstocks that seem implausible and cannot be verified by auditors.

The direction of travel is clear: more checking, and more of it falling on buyers who thought the certificate was enough.

## A delivery note is not a Proof of Sustainability

Fuel distributors see the same misunderstanding again and again: a sheet showing how many litres of HVO or B100 you bought is not a Proof of Sustainability. A valid PoS carries specific information, including the certified feedstock and its origin, and it is tied to the consignment. Companies preparing for FuelEU Maritime, ESG reporting or RED II and RED III compliance can discover too late that the document they hold does not meet the formal requirements.

If you take one thing from this post, take that: check what you actually received. (For background on how the certificates themselves work, see our guide to [ISCC certification](/blog/iscc-certification-explained/).)

## The seven questions

**1. Is the certificate genuine, current and in scope?**
Look the supplier up in the certification scheme's public database. Check the expiry date, the legal entity, and whether the certificate covers the activity being sold: collecting, trading or processing.

**2. Does each consignment come with its own Proof of Sustainability?**
Not a summary, not a delivery note. Ask for a sample document before the first order, and check it states the feedstock and its origin.

**3. Where exactly does the feedstock come from?**
Ask for the collection points or mills behind the cargo. A supplier who can name them and explain how material moves between them is in a different position from one who offers only a country.

**4. Do the volumes add up?**
Compare the quantity offered with what the named sources could realistically produce. Fraud cases were exposed precisely because the declared volumes stopped being plausible. This is the cheapest check on the list, and often the most revealing.

**5. How is the mass balance kept?**
Mass balance is the bookkeeping that tracks certified material through mixed streams, and tighter verification of it sits at the centre of the certifiers' response to fraud. Ask to see how the supplier records inputs and outputs, and how often it reconciles them.

**6. What does the audit history show?**
Ask when the supplier was last audited, by whom, and whether any findings were raised. Check the public database for suspended or withdrawn certificates linked to the company or its upstream partners.

**7. Will they support your own checks?**
A confident supplier will welcome a site visit, accept a third-party audit and keep retained samples. Chemical testing methods are being developed because paper alone has proven insufficient, so a supplier open to testing is ahead of the curve.

## What to do with the answers

No single answer settles the matter. A new supplier may have a thin audit history but excellent records; an established one may have a clean certificate and a vague account of where its oil comes from. What you are building is a picture, and three habits help:

- **Record what you asked and when.** If a question is raised later, a dated file is your best protection.
- **Repeat the checks.** Certificates expire and circumstances change. Treat due diligence as a routine, not a one-off.
- **Look upstream.** Large buyers increasingly ask for visibility down to their suppliers' suppliers. You do not need their scale to adopt the habit.

A caution on tone: none of this means treating every supplier as a suspect. Most are exactly what they say they are. The point is that when the market is being tested, honest suppliers benefit from buyers who ask clear questions, because clear questions are easy to answer.
