For years, used cooking oil (UCO) was an afterthought in the commodity world — a waste stream that kitchens disposed of and recyclers quietly handled.

That is no longer the case.

Regulatory frameworks across Europe, Asia and the Middle East have transformed UCO from a waste product into one of the most sought-after feedstocks in the clean energy transition. And the supply — by its very nature — cannot scale as fast as the mandates demand.

This is the market SafOil operates in, and it is moving faster than most buyers and sellers have planned for.

## What is driving demand — and why it is accelerating in 2026

The primary driver is Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF). UCO is the dominant feedstock for the HEFA-SPK production pathway, which currently accounts for approximately 85% of all SAF produced globally. As airline operators face increasing regulatory pressure to blend SAF into their fuel supply, demand for UCO as a production input has intensified dramatically.

The regulatory pipeline is clear and binding. The EU's **ReFuelEU Aviation** initiative mandates a minimum 6% SAF blending requirement by 2030, scaling to 70% by 2050. **Singapore** introduced a 1% SAF requirement effective January 2026, with targets rising to 3–5% by 2030. And **Thailand** introduced a 1% SAF mandate for 2026 — significant for a country that sits at the intersection of major aviation routes and regional UCO supply chains.

Biodiesel mandates are adding further pressure. Indonesia's **B50** biodiesel target for 2026 is redirecting significant domestic feedstock away from export channels. For buyers who had built supply strategies around Indonesian UCO volumes, this represents a meaningful supply-side shift.

The numbers reflect the scale of the shift: the global UCO market was valued at approximately USD 17.2 billion in 2025, and is projected to reach USD 36.9 billion by 2036 — a compound annual growth rate of 7.2% across a decade. Asia-Pacific is the primary growth engine, with a CAGR of over 7% projected through 2031.

## The structural problem: mandates are outpacing supply

Here is the tension at the centre of this market.

UCO supply is, by definition, a function of how much cooking oil is consumed and how efficiently it is collected. You cannot manufacture more of it on demand. Collection infrastructure, regional concentration and traceability requirements all constrain how quickly the available supply base can be expanded.

Regulatory mandates, on the other hand, operate on their own timeline.

The result is a widening structural gap between what compliance frameworks require and what the physical supply chain can actually deliver. For buyers — fuel producers, airlines, biodiesel refiners — this gap translates directly into price pressure, sourcing uncertainty and a growing premium on reliable, documented supply relationships.

Feedstock authenticity is also an emerging concern. As UCO prices have risen with demand, documentation integrity — the traceability of feedstock from kitchen to refinery — has become a procurement priority, not merely a compliance checkbox.
